An experiment in running a company of AI agents

One human CEO. A company of AI agents. How far does that stretch?

CaliperForge is run by AI agents with defined roles, rules, SOPs, review gates and chains of command, under one human. What the company produces is engineering and conformance testing for crypto payment infrastructure: checking that implementations do what their specs say. We log all of it, daily.

The experiment

A company where the staff are agents

Not a chatbot with a task list. A structure: roles with charters, a rulebook the agents enforce on each other, review gates between writing and shipping, written channels for who may talk to whom, and one human at the top who sets direction and signs off. The question is how much one person's capacity can be stretched, and what that person has to learn to do it.

ROLES

Seats with a job

A COO, an orchestrator and a fixer, language-specialist builders, and reviewers. Every run is attributed to a seat, so work is measurable per role.

RULES

106 rules, enforced

Lessons become rules, and rules become checks in code. Pre-review rails alone have run 65,393 times.

HUMAN

One CEO, few decisions

Eleven deliverables needed explicit approval. Everything else passes the gates or gets refused by them.

How a job travels

Nothing ships without passing five gates

Every change starts as a written brief and ends as a pull request. In between, it is built by a language specialist and checked by automatic rails, two separate reviewers, and a final proof. A refusal is not a failure; it sends the job back with a reason.

What changed itWhat we saw
Review gates and a conflict fixOne loop on a stale branch burned 7.76M tokens in a single night before it was found. Rework runs were 36% of all tokens in the first week.
A faster tickJobs spent 53.9 hours waiting between their own runs against 19.7 hours of model time. The tick went from five minutes to one.
Packets with a capEach seat reads a derived, capped brief instead of exploring the repository.
Read this before quoting the headline. Most of the drop is in cache reads, which went from 7.05M to about 1.6M per job; fresh tokens barely moved. We also measured our own boot at 128,779 tokens before any work, and the easy cuts were worth about 11%. We said then it was not a step change, and it was not.

The measurement we don't have yet: human capacity

The core claim of the experiment is how far one person stretches. Today we can count the machine's work but not the human's attention per unit of it.
